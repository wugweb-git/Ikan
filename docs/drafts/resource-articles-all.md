## 1. "5 Minutes That Can Change How You Handle Stress"

**Format:** Video | **Category:** Stress | **Est. length:** 4 min watch
**Source:** NHS Every Mind Matters (official NHS channel)
**Video link:** https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z7ATOc4ShOc

> Stress shows up as racing thoughts, a tight chest, snapping at people you don't mean to snap at, lying awake re-running a conversation from nine hours ago. Most advice for dealing with it asks for things you don't have on a bad day — an hour, a quiet room, a plan, a willingness to "just relax." This is built around the opposite assumption: that the only resource you reliably have on a stressful day is five minutes.

### What stress is actually doing to you

Stress isn't only a mental state — it's a physical one. Your body responds to a looming deadline much the same way it responds to a genuine threat: heart rate up, breathing shallower and higher in the chest, muscles — especially the shoulders and jaw — tensed and ready for something that, in a work context, usually never comes. Left running for long enough, that physical state starts affecting sleep, digestion, concentration and mood, independent of whatever triggered it in the first place.

This is also why "just relax" doesn't work as advice: it asks you to override a physical state with a thought, which is roughly backwards. It's far more effective to change the physical state directly — slower breathing, released muscle tension, a change of physical position — and let your mental state follow, which it reliably does.

### Why five minutes is enough to matter

You don't need to resolve what's stressing you to feel less wound up by it. A short, deliberate action — a few slow breaths, a brief walk, consciously dropping your shoulders — can measurably lower that physical stress response in minutes, well before the actual problem is anywhere close to solved. The goal isn't to fix the stressor. It's to stop your body from treating an email as a predator.

### What's in the video

This short video from the NHS's Every Mind Matters programme walks through a handful of things you can actually do in the moment — practical, not theoretical, and built for people who don't have a spare hour or a meditation app subscription.

**[VIDEO EMBEDS HERE]**

### Three ways to make this stick

- **Pick one technique, not all of them.** Trying to adopt five new habits in one afternoon is its own source of stress. One, used consistently, beats five used once.
- **Use it early, not just at the worst moment.** The next time you notice your shoulders creeping up toward your ears — not just when you're already overwhelmed — is the better time to practice.
- **Name the stressor, in one sentence, out loud or in writing.** Vague stress ("everything") is much harder to act on than a specific one ("this one email I'm avoiding").

### If five minutes isn't enough

Some stress is situational and passes once the situation does. Some is more persistent, and worth actually measuring rather than guessing at — the Stress assessment in this app takes about four minutes and gives you a clearer read on where you stand than "I think I'm pretty stressed lately" does. And if this resonates more broadly, Every Mind Matters has a free personalised action plan at nhs.uk/every-mind-matters — five questions, a plan built around your answers.

## 2. "Listen: A 5-Minute Guided Breathing Exercise for Anxious Moments"

**Format:** Audio | **Category:** Mental Health | **Est. length:** 6 min listen
**Source:** Mindful Awareness Research Centre (MARC), UCLA, via The Free Mindfulness Project
**Audio file:** https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1eucLhrVRBT7FCTzdbpns7MCLb4PILK0t
**Source page:** https://www.freemindfulness.org/download
**License:** Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial-ShareAlike 3.0

> Sometimes the fastest way back to calm isn't thinking your way there — it's paying attention to your breath for a few minutes, on purpose. It sounds too simple to matter. It also happens to work, which is a large part of why guided breathing shows up across anxiety research, clinical therapy and mindfulness practice alike, despite those fields not agreeing on much else.

### Why breath, specifically

Breathing is one of the few bodily processes that runs on its own but that you can also take deliberate control of at any moment. That makes it a rare, direct lever: slowing your breath down on purpose is one of the most reliable ways to signal to your nervous system that you're not in danger right now — which, in an anxious moment, is often most of what's actually needed before anything else gets solved. You can't will your heart rate down directly. You can, with practice, do it through your breath.

### What this is — and isn't

This isn't a breathing "technique" with counts to memorize or a method you can get wrong. It's a guided practice: a recorded voice doing the pacing for you, so the only thing you need to bring is five minutes and a willingness to let your attention rest somewhere other than whatever's been looping in your head.

### Before you press play

- Find five minutes where you won't be interrupted — mid-task doesn't work as well as a real pause between things.
- Sit or lie down somewhere you can let your shoulders drop without holding yourself up.
- Headphones help with focus but aren't required.

This recording is a simple, guided breathing practice: no background music, no jargon, just a clear voice bringing your attention back to the breath whenever it wanders — and it will wander. That's normal, not a sign you're doing it wrong. Noticing that your mind drifted and gently returning is, if anything, the actual practice.

**[AUDIO EMBEDS HERE]**

### After the practice

Notice what's different, even slightly — a slower heart rate, looser shoulders, a quieter head, a few seconds of actual stillness before the next thought arrives. You don't need a dramatic shift for this to be working. A small one, repeated regularly rather than reached for only in a crisis, is the actual mechanism — this works better as a daily few minutes than as a once-a-month emergency measure.

### Where this fits

A single five-minute practice won't resolve ongoing anxiety any more than one gym session builds fitness. Used consistently, though, it's a genuinely well-supported way to lower your baseline — worth pairing with an honest look at where you actually stand, which is what this app's assessments are for.

### What Happens When Stress Takes Over?

**Presentation:** Watch
**Category:** Stress & Resilience
**Author:** National Institute of Mental Health
**Source:** NIMH
**Duration:** ~10 min
**Media:** Video

**Video URL:**
[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Dq9odPtHbcg&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Source page:**
[NIMH — Guided Visualization: Dealing with Stress](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/news/media/2021/guided-visualization-dealing-with-stress?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

NIMH's page identifies the video as **Guided Visualization: Dealing with Stress** and provides the complete transcript. It explains how stress affects the brain and then guides the viewer through a visualization exercise. [National Institute of Mental Health](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/news/media/2021/guided-visualization-dealing-with-stress)

### Article

Stress is a normal part of life. A difficult deadline, an important decision, an unfamiliar situation or a major change can all activate the body's stress response.

That response exists for a reason.

When your brain detects a potential threat, your body can rapidly shift into a state of increased alertness. Your heart rate can change, your breathing can become faster, and your muscles can prepare you to respond.

For a short period, this can be useful.

The problem is not that the stress response exists. The problem can arise when the response stays activated for too long or is triggered repeatedly.

## Stress changes how you experience the moment

When you are under pressure, your attention can become focused on the immediate problem.

That can make it harder to step back, think through alternatives or make decisions calmly.

This is part of the body's protective response. The brain is prioritising what it perceives as important right now.

You might notice this as physical tension, a racing heart, changes in breathing, tiredness, difficulty concentrating or feeling constantly "on edge."

Stress can therefore be both a mental and physical experience.

## Your brain is responding to a signal

One part of the brain involved in the stress response is the amygdala, which helps process emotional responses to potential threats.

Other areas of the brain, including the prefrontal cortex, are involved in planning, problem-solving and decision-making.

When you are repeatedly under stress, it can become harder to feel calm enough to think through a situation.

That does not mean your brain is damaged every time you feel stressed.

It means that prolonged or repeated stress can affect how your body and mind feel and function.

## You do not need to eliminate stress

The goal is not to create a life without stress.

That is unrealistic.

Instead, it can be useful to recognise when your system has been under pressure for too long and deliberately create opportunities to recover.

That might mean stepping away from a demanding task, changing your environment, talking to someone, getting enough sleep, moving your body or using a relaxation technique.

Different approaches work for different people.

## Try creating a mental "safe place"

One technique explored by NIMH is guided visualization.

The basic idea is simple: bring your attention deliberately to a place or scene that feels calm and safe.

You can imagine a real place you know or create one in your mind.

Focus on details.

What can you see?

What can you hear?

What does the air feel like?

What colours are around you?

What does the ground feel like beneath your feet?

The purpose is not to pretend that the source of your stress has disappeared.

It is to give your attention something different to focus on for a period of time.

## Breathing gives you another anchor

Slow, deliberate breathing can also help you focus your attention on the present moment.

Rather than trying to force yourself to stop thinking, simply notice your breathing.

Breathe in.

Breathe out.

Notice the physical sensation.

If your attention wanders, bring it back.

There is no requirement to do this perfectly.

## When stress becomes persistent

Occasional stress is different from feeling continuously overwhelmed.

If stress is persistent, increasingly difficult to manage, affecting your sleep or concentration, interfering with everyday activities, or causing significant distress, it may be worth discussing what you are experiencing with a qualified professional.

An assessment can help structure that conversation, but a score alone cannot explain why you are experiencing stress.

The useful question is not simply:

**"How stressed am I?"**

It is:

**"What is my stress doing to my life, and what would help?"**

The answer may begin with something small.

Recognising the signal is already a useful first step.

### Practical Ways to Manage Stress and Anxiety

**Presentation:** Watch
**Category:** Stress & Resilience
**Author:** National Institute of Mental Health
**Source:** NIMH
**Duration:** Short video

**Video URL:**
[Watch on YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=RIyge4jzeFg&utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Source page:**
[NIMH — GREAT: Helpful Practices to Manage Stress and Anxiety](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/news/media/2021/great-helpful-practices-to-manage-stress-and-anxiety?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

The NIMH resource presents the **GREAT** framework: Gratitude, Relaxation, Exercise, Acknowledge feelings and Track thoughts. [National Institute of Mental Health](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/news/media/2021/great-helpful-practices-to-manage-stress-and-anxiety)

### Article

Stress management does not have to begin with a complete change to your life.

Often, the first useful step is understanding what is happening and finding one practical way to respond.

Stress and anxiety can affect how you think, feel and behave. When they become persistent, they can also interfere with sleep, concentration, relationships and everyday activities.

The National Institute of Mental Health uses a simple framework called GREAT to introduce several practical approaches to managing stress and anxiety.

## G — Gratitude

Gratitude does not mean ignoring difficult experiences.

It means deliberately noticing something positive or meaningful alongside whatever is difficult.

That could be something very small: a person you appreciate, a place where you feel comfortable, something you accomplished, or a part of your day that went better than expected.

The point is not to convince yourself that everything is fine.

It is to broaden your attention.

When stress dominates your attention, deliberately noticing something else can create a little more psychological space.

## R — Relaxation

Your body needs opportunities to move out of a heightened state of alertness.

Relaxation can take many forms.

You might use slow breathing, progressive muscle relaxation, guided imagery, meditation or simply spend a few minutes somewhere quiet.

There is no requirement to empty your mind.

The objective is to give your body and attention an opportunity to settle.

Even a short pause can be useful when repeated consistently.

## E — Exercise

Physical activity can be another way of supporting your mental wellbeing.

Exercise does not have to mean an intensive workout.

Walking, stretching, cycling, playing a sport or simply moving more throughout the day can all be ways to introduce physical activity.

The useful activity is the one that fits your circumstances and that you can realistically continue.

Consistency generally matters more than trying to do everything at once.

## A — Acknowledge your feelings

Trying to suppress an uncomfortable feeling can sometimes make it harder to understand.

Instead, give yourself a moment to identify what you are experiencing.

Are you worried?

Frustrated?

Overwhelmed?

Angry?

Sad?

Exhausted?

You do not need to immediately solve the feeling.

Naming it can create some distance between the experience and your reaction to it.

It can also help you identify what might be contributing to it.

## T — Track your thoughts

Stress can create repetitive thinking.

You may find yourself repeatedly imagining what could go wrong, replaying something that happened or anticipating a future problem.

Writing these thoughts down can make them easier to examine.

Ask:

**What am I thinking?**

**What triggered the thought?**

**Is this something I can influence?**

**What would I tell someone else in the same situation?**

You do not need to challenge every thought.

The objective is to notice patterns rather than automatically reacting to every thought as if it were a fact.

## Put the pieces together

These strategies work best when they become practical behaviours rather than another checklist you feel pressured to complete.

You might start with one.

Take a short walk.

Write down what is occupying your mind.

Spend five minutes breathing slowly.

Call someone you trust.

Notice one thing that went well today.

Then see what changes.

## When self-management is not enough

Stress and anxiety are common experiences, but persistent or severe symptoms deserve attention.

If what you are experiencing continues to interfere with your everyday life, relationships, sleep, work or ability to function, consider talking to a qualified healthcare or mental health professional.

Self-care is not a replacement for professional support when professional support is needed.

## Start smaller than you think

When you are overwhelmed, a large improvement plan can itself become another source of pressure.

Choose one action.

Do it today.

Then decide what comes next.

Managing stress is rarely about finding one perfect technique.

It is about building enough awareness and support to respond to what you are experiencing rather than simply pushing through it.

[Samhsa](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/children-and-families/coping-resources/multimedia?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### Article

Sometimes the hardest part of getting support is starting the conversation.

You may know that something does not feel right, but not know how to explain it.

You may worry about being judged.

You may think your problem is not serious enough.

Or you may simply not know what to say.

You do not need to have the right words before you start.

## A conversation does not require a diagnosis

Talking about mental health does not mean deciding what condition someone has.

It can simply mean saying what you have noticed.

"I haven't been sleeping well."

"I've been feeling overwhelmed."

"I don't feel like myself lately."

"I'm finding it harder to concentrate."

"I've been struggling and I don't really know why."

These are enough to begin.

A conversation can happen before there is a diagnosis, before there is an assessment result and before you know exactly what kind of support you need.

## Start with what you have noticed

If you are talking to someone about your own mental health, describe your experience rather than trying to explain it perfectly.

Think about changes.

Has your mood changed?

Has your sleep changed?

Are you avoiding things you normally do?

Are you finding work or study more difficult?

Are you withdrawing from people?

Are you feeling more anxious, low, irritable or overwhelmed?

Specific observations can make a conversation easier.

## Listening matters as much as talking

If someone approaches you about their mental health, you do not need to immediately solve the problem.

Start by listening.

Ask what they are experiencing.

Give them time.

Avoid turning the conversation into an argument about whether their feelings are justified.

You can say:

**"I'm here to listen."**

**"Do you want to tell me what has been happening?"**

**"What would be helpful right now?"**

**"Would you like help finding someone to talk to?"**

These questions leave room for the other person to describe what they need.

## You can offer help without taking control

Supporting someone does not mean becoming responsible for solving their mental health.

You can help them find information.

You can help them arrange an appointment.

You can offer practical support.

You can check in.

But it is also reasonable to have boundaries.

Support works better when everyone understands what they can realistically provide.

## An assessment can help start the conversation

Sometimes it is easier to talk about a result than to explain an experience from scratch.

An assessment can give you something concrete to bring into the conversation.

You might say:

**"I completed this assessment and the result concerned me. Can we talk about what I've been experiencing?"**

The result is not the conclusion.

It is a starting point.

A qualified professional can consider the result alongside your symptoms, history, circumstances and other relevant information.

## Asking for help is an action

You do not have to wait until you reach a crisis before asking for support.

If something has been bothering you for a while, talking to someone can be a useful next step.

That person might be someone you trust.

It might be a doctor, therapist, counsellor or another qualified professional.

The appropriate person depends on what you are experiencing and the kind of support you need.

## If the conversation is difficult

Not every conversation will go perfectly.

Someone may not be ready to talk.

They may become uncomfortable.

They may not know what they need.

That does not necessarily mean the conversation failed.

You can leave the door open.

**"You don't have to talk about it now. I'm here if you want to later."**

Sometimes knowing that someone is willing to listen is the first form of support.

## Start with three words

You do not need a speech.

You can simply ask:

**Can we talk?**

Sometimes that is enough to open the door.

### Mental Health Isn't Always Visible

**Presentation:** Listen
**Category:** Mental Health Awareness
**Author:** SAMHSA
**Source:** U.S. Department of Health & Human Services / SAMHSA
**Duration:** 30 sec / 20 sec

**Audio/media page:**
[SAMHSA Mental Health Multimedia Resources](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/children-and-families/coping-resources/multimedia?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

**Important:** The SAMHSA page provides the actual MP3 download controls for its audio resources and confirms that the material is free for public use. [Samhsa](https://www.samhsa.gov/mental-health/children-and-families/coping-resources/multimedia?utm_source=chatgpt.com)

### Article

You cannot always tell how someone is doing by looking at them.

Someone can appear productive, social and completely in control while privately struggling with stress, anxiety, low mood or exhaustion.

Mental health does not always have an obvious outward sign.

That is one reason conversations matter.

## Not everything shows on the outside

Physical health problems can sometimes be visible.

Mental health experiences can be much less obvious.

Someone may continue going to work while struggling to sleep.

They may attend meetings while finding it difficult to concentrate.

They may keep up with responsibilities while feeling overwhelmed.

They may laugh with friends while privately experiencing something very different.

This does not mean that every change in behaviour indicates a mental health problem.

It means that appearances alone cannot tell us the complete story.

## Functioning does not tell you everything

Being able to work does not necessarily mean someone is doing well.

Likewise, having a difficult day does not automatically mean someone has a mental health condition.

Human experiences exist on a spectrum.

What matters is the broader pattern.

How long has the experience lasted?

How intense is it?

Is it getting worse?

Is it interfering with everyday life?

Is the person able to do the things that matter to them?

Are they asking for help?

These questions provide more useful context than appearances alone.

## Why measurement can help

One reason structured assessments can be useful is that they create a way to report experiences that may otherwise remain invisible.

Instead of simply saying "I'm fine," a person can answer questions about specific experiences.

How often are you having difficulty sleeping?

How often are you feeling nervous or overwhelmed?

How difficult is it to concentrate?

How much are these experiences affecting everyday life?

The resulting score provides structured information.

It does not tell the whole story.

But it can make an invisible experience easier to discuss.

## A score is not a label

A mental health assessment should not turn into a judgement about a person.

A score represents responses to a defined set of questions.

It does not measure someone's character.

It does not measure their worth.

It does not tell you whether they are strong or weak.

And a screening result alone does not establish a diagnosis.

The useful purpose of measurement is to identify patterns, track change and support better conversations.

## Notice change

Sometimes the most useful information is not the absolute score but the change over time.

A person may notice that they are sleeping less.

Their concentration may be getting worse.

They may be withdrawing from friends.

They may feel increasingly overwhelmed.

These changes can be worth paying attention to even before there is a clear explanation for them.

The same principle applies when things improve.

A change in score, symptoms or everyday functioning can help show whether something may be helping.

## Make room for conversation

If you are concerned about someone, you do not need to diagnose them.

You can ask.

**"How have you actually been doing?"**

**"I've noticed you've seemed under a lot of pressure lately. Do you want to talk?"**

**"Is there anything I can do?"**

Then listen.

You do not need to immediately provide an answer.

## Support can start before certainty

You do not have to know exactly what is happening before you can seek support.

If you are struggling, you can say that you are struggling.

If you are worried about someone else, you can let them know that you are available.

If an assessment result concerns you, you can bring it to a qualified professional and ask what it means in the context of your experience.

Mental health does not need to become visible to everyone before it deserves attention.

Sometimes the first useful step is simply making the invisible easier to talk about.
